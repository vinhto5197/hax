# The Nevron Language: A Complete Grammar

Nevron is a constructed language designed around a single principle: everything about
it can be deduced from a fixed set of rules. It is agglutinative, meaning that words are
assembled by attaching suffixes to roots, and each suffix carries exactly one meaning.
There are no irregular nouns and no irregular verbs. Because grammatical roles are marked
on the words themselves and the word order is fixed, the structure of any sentence can
always be recovered. The vocabulary is invented and does not borrow from natural
languages, so a speaker cannot fall back on guesswork from other tongues.

This document describes the sound system, the way words are formed, the order in which
they combine, the core vocabulary, and a set of worked examples. Read in order, it gives
everything needed to both understand and produce new Nevron sentences.

## Sounds and Spelling

Nevron has five vowels, written a, e, i, o, and u, each pronounced as a single pure
sound. It also has four diphthongs, written ae, ei, ai, and au, each gliding from the
first vowel toward the second. The consonants are p, b, t, d, k, g, m, n, l, r, s, z, h,
and v, together with the digraph "th," which is pronounced like the th in the English
word "thin." Spelling is entirely regular: every letter is always pronounced, and there
are no silent letters.

Stress is predictable and never marked in writing. It always falls on the second-to-last
syllable of a word. When suffixes are added and a word grows longer, the stress shifts
accordingly, always remaining on the penultimate syllable.

## The Linking Rule

One sound rule governs how suffixes attach, and it is worth learning early because it
applies throughout the grammar. When a stem that ends in a vowel meets a suffix that
begins with a vowel, the consonant "v" is inserted between them. This keeps two vowels
from colliding. Because every case suffix in Nevron begins with a vowel, any noun or
pronoun that ends in a vowel will show this inserted "v" before its case ending.

For example, the noun "vora," meaning water, takes the accusative suffix "-en" to become
"voraven," not "voraen." The pronoun "mi," meaning I, takes the genitive suffix "-ul" to
become "mivul," meaning my. The noun "renu," meaning name, becomes "renuva" in the
nominative. Stems that already end in a consonant simply take the suffix directly, with
no inserted "v": the noun "dren," meaning house, becomes "drena" in the nominative.

## Nouns and the Case System

Every Nevron noun changes its ending to show its role in the sentence. There are seven
cases, and each one is a single suffix with a single function. The endings are the same
for every noun, with the only variation being the inserted "v" required by the linking
rule for vowel-final stems.

| Case | Suffix | Meaning | Example with "dren" (house) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | -a | the subject, the doer | drena |
| Accusative | -en | the direct object | drenen |
| Genitive | -ul | possession, "of" or "'s" | drenul |
| Dative | -or | recipient, "to" or "for"; motion toward | drenor |
| Locative | -is | location, "in," "at," "on" | drenis |
| Ablative | -ed | source, "from," "out of" | drened |
| Comitative | -eth | accompaniment, "with" | dreneth |

The nominative, ending in "-a," marks the subject of the clause, the one performing the
action. The accusative, ending in "-en," marks the direct object, the thing the action is
done to. The genitive, ending in "-ul," shows possession and corresponds to the English
"of" or apostrophe-s; the possessor always comes before the thing possessed, so "saelul
drena" means "the friend's house." The dative, ending in "-or," marks the indirect object,
the recipient to whom or for whom something is done, and it also expresses motion toward a
destination, so "karnor" means "to the city." The locative, ending in "-is," places
something in, at, or on a location, so "drenis" means "in the house." The ablative, ending
in "-ed," shows the source or origin of motion, so "karned" means "from the city." Finally
the comitative, ending in "-eth," expresses accompaniment and means "with," so "saeleth"
means "with a friend."

Remember that vowel-final nouns apply the linking rule before any of these endings. The
noun "vora," water, becomes "voraven" in the accusative, "voravis" in the locative, and
"voraveth" in the comitative.

## Showing More Than One

To make a noun plural, add the suffix "-ir." This suffix attaches directly to the root,
before any case ending, so the full order inside a noun is root, then plural, then case.
The friend is "sael"; friends is "saelir"; friends as a subject is "saelira"; and friends
as an object is "saeliren." Because "-ir" begins with a consonant, the linking rule never
applies before it.

There is one situation where the plural suffix is deliberately left off. When a number
word stands in front of a noun, the noun stays singular, because the number itself already
expresses how many there are. So "two friends" is "kel saela," using the singular "saela"
rather than the plural form. The numbers from one to ten are ot, kel, thon, pel, nim, sed,
rav, bral, grel, and sath.

## Pronouns

The personal pronouns are short words that, like nouns, can take case endings. As
subjects they are used bare, with no ending at all. The subject forms are "mi" for I,
"ti" for you in the singular, "si" for he, she, or it, "mei" for we, "tei" for you in the
plural, and "sei" for they.

When a pronoun plays any role other than the subject, it takes the linking "v" followed by
the appropriate case suffix, exactly as a vowel-final noun would. So the object forms are
miven, tiven, siven, meiven, teiven, and seiven, meaning me, you, him or her, us, you all,
and them. The possessive forms use the genitive ending and are mivul, tivul, sivul,
meivul, teivul, and seivul, meaning my, your, his or her, our, your, and their.

## Verbs

A Nevron verb is built in a fixed sequence. First comes an optional negation prefix, then
the verb root, then a tense suffix, and finally a subject-agreement suffix. Nothing else
goes inside the verb, and the pieces never change shape.

There are three tenses, each a single suffix that comes immediately after the root. The
present tense is "-i," the past tense is "-uth," and the future tense is "-ael." So from
the root "lan," meaning see, we get "lani" for the present stem, "lanuth" for the past
stem, and "lanael" for the future stem.

After the tense comes the agreement suffix, which tells us who the subject is. The elegant
part of the system is that the agreement suffix is identical to the subject pronoun. The
endings are therefore "-mi," "-ti," "-si," "-mei," "-tei," and "-sei" for the six persons.
Putting it together, "lanimi" means I see, "lanuthti" means you saw, and "lanaelsei" means
they will see.

To make a verb negative, attach the prefix "na-" to the very front. So "drossimi" means I
want, and "nadrossimi" means I do not want. Likewise "lanisi" means he sees, and "nalanisi"
means he does not see.

The verb "to be" is completely regular. Its root is "es," and it conjugates like any other
verb: "esimi" means I am, "esisi" means he, she, or it is, and "esisei" means they are. A
past form such as "esuthsi" means he or she was. When the copula links a subject to a noun
or an adjective, that following noun or adjective appears in its plain nominative or bare
form rather than taking any object ending.

## Adjectives, Demonstratives, and Comparison

Adjectives in Nevron do not change their form, and they always stand directly in front of
the noun they describe. So "nev drena" means a new house, with "nev" meaning new and
"drena" meaning house. Demonstratives behave the same way and also precede the noun: "sen"
means this and "ron" means that, so "sen drena" is this house and "ron warna" is that dog.

Comparison is handled with two suffixes on the adjective. The comparative, meaning "more,"
is "-an," so "orn," meaning big, becomes "ornan," meaning bigger. The superlative, meaning
"most," is "-ant," so "orn" becomes "ornant," meaning biggest. The same pattern works for
any adjective, so "bron," good, gives "bronan," better, and "bronant," best.

## Putting Words in Order

Nevron places the verb at the end of the clause. The basic order is subject, then object,
then verb, often abbreviated as SOV. When a sentence also contains an indirect object or
another oblique phrase, that phrase comes between the subject and the direct object, giving
the fuller order of subject, then dative or oblique phrase, then direct object, then verb.
Any adverb sits immediately before the verb.

Inside a noun phrase, every modifier comes before the noun it belongs to, and the
modifiers themselves follow a set order: first a demonstrative or a number, then a genitive
possessor, then an adjective, and finally the noun. This means a phrase like "my big house"
unfolds as possessor, adjective, noun.

## Asking Questions

A yes-or-no question keeps the ordinary statement word order and simply adds the particle
"ku" at the very end of the sentence. So "ti sirelen laniti ku" asks "Do you see the bird?"
The natural replies are "ja" for yes and "nav" for no.

A content question, the kind that asks who, what, where, and so on, uses a question word
placed in the ordinary position of whatever it is asking about. Nothing moves to the front
of the sentence. The question word takes the same case and number endings as any noun in
that role. The question words are "ken" for who, "kesh" for what, "kis" for where, "keth"
for when, "kor" for why, and "kun" for how. For example, "ti keshen gruniti" means "What do
you eat?", where "kesh" carries the accusative ending "-en" because it stands in the object
slot.

## Connecting Words

A few small words join clauses and phrases together. "vo" means and, "kai" means or, "tor"
means but, and "sond" means because. The particle "ku" marks a yes-or-no question and
always comes last, while "ja" and "nav" mean yes and no.

## Core Vocabulary

The following roots are enough to build a wide range of sentences. Nouns are given in their
bare root form, to which case and number endings attach. Among the common nouns are noren,
meaning person; sael, friend; dren, house; vora, water; mesk, food; lirn, book; orun, tree;
karn, city; aven, sky; thur, sun; and lekt, word. Further nouns include renu, meaning name;
veth, road; sirel, bird; dalm, hand; harn, fire; gron, stone; kuno, child; warn, dog; miel,
river; thal, day; and murn, night.

The verbs are also given as roots, ready to take a tense and an agreement ending. They
include lan, meaning see; grun, eat; sup, drink; vael, go; nuth, come; tesk, speak or say;
ven, know; and dross, want. More verbs are dael, meaning give; forn, make or do; kael,
have; relm, love; drev, sleep; skan, read; grav, write; and es, the verb to be.

The adjectives, which never change form, include orn, meaning big; pik, small; bron, good;
mor, bad; frest, cold; nev, new; grus, old; ralk, red; lume, happy; and thav, warm.

## Worked Examples

The sentence "mi saelen lanimi" means "I see the friend." It breaks down as "mi," I, the
subject, then "saelen," the friend in the accusative as the object, then "lanimi," the verb
see in the present tense with first-person singular agreement. This shows the basic
subject-object-verb order at its simplest.

The sentence "ti mesken grunuthti" means "You ate the food." Here "ti" is you, "mesken" is
food in the accusative, and "grunuthti" is the verb eat in the past tense agreeing with a
second-person singular subject.

The sentence "sei karnor vaelaelsei" means "They will go to the city." The subject "sei"
means they, "karnor" puts city in the dative to express motion toward it, and "vaelaelsei"
is the verb go in the future tense agreeing with a third-person plural subject.

The sentence "mi voraven nadrossimi" means "I do not want water." The object "voraven" is
water in the accusative, showing the inserted linking "v," and the verb "nadrossimi" carries
the negation prefix "na-" in front of want in the present tense with first-person singular
agreement.

The sentence "lirna drenis esisi" means "The book is in the house." The subject "lirna" is
book in the nominative, "drenis" is house in the locative to mean "in the house," and
"esisi" is the copula in the present tense agreeing with a third-person singular subject.

The sentence "mivul saelul drena orn esisi" means "My friend's house is big." It stacks two
genitives, "mivul" for my and "saelul" for friend's, in front of "drena," the house in the
nominative, followed by the bare adjective "orn," big, and the copula "esisi."

The sentence "saelira lume esisei" means "The friends are happy." The subject "saelira" is
friend with the plural suffix and the nominative ending, "lume" is the bare adjective happy,
and "esisei" is the copula agreeing with a third-person plural subject.

The sentence "kel saela lirnen skanisei" means "Two friends read a book." The number "kel,"
two, keeps the following noun singular as "saela," the object "lirnen" is book in the
accusative, and "skanisei" is the verb read agreeing with a third-person plural subject.

## A Short Reading Passage

The following short passage uses only the vocabulary and rules above, and can be read
straight through. In Nevron it runs: "sen thal lume esisi. mei karnor vaelimei. mei karnis
noreniren lanimei. mei mesken grunimei vo voraven supimei. murnis mei drenor nuthimei."

Translated into English, the passage reads: "This is a happy day. We go to the city. In the
city we see people. We eat food and drink water. At night we come to the house." Each
sentence follows the patterns already described: the demonstrative and adjective sit before
their nouns, the locative and dative express place and direction, the conjunction "vo" links
two clauses, and every verb closes its clause with a present-tense stem and a first-person
plural agreement ending.
